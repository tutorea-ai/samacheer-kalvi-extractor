Teacher explains — Part 1: What is Genetics? (English):
"Genetics is the branch of biological science which deals with the mechanism of transmission of characters from parents to offspring. Think of it as the science of inheritance — how you got your mother's dimples or your father's height. The term 'Genetics' was introduced by W. Bateson in 1906. Now, Genetics is not just one thing — it has four major sub-disciplines. First: Transmission Genetics, also called Classical Genetics — this deals with how genes are transmitted from parents to offspring. The foundation of Classical Genetics came from Gregor Mendel's study of seven genes in pea plants, which we will learn deeply in this unit. Second: Molecular Genetics — this deals with the structure and function of a gene at the molecular level — think of it as zooming into the gene itself. Third: Population Genetics — this deals with heredity in groups of individuals for traits determined by a few genes. Fourth: Quantitative Genetics — this deals with heredity of traits governed by many genes simultaneously in a group. Write these four names in your notebook now."

Teacher explains — Part 2: Heredity and Variation (English):
"Genetics is often described as the science of heredity and variation. Heredity is the transmission of characters from parents to offspring — it is why you share features with your parents. Variation is different — it refers to the differences in characteristics shown by organisms belonging to the same natural population or species. So in a class of 40 students, you all belong to the same species, yet each of you is different in height, skin colour, hair type. That difference is variation. Now, variation has two types. Type one is Discontinuous Variation. Here, within a population, characteristics show only a limited form of variation — there is no in-between form. Examples from the chapter: style length in Primula, and plant height in garden pea — a pea plant is either tall or dwarf, nothing in between. These variations are genetically determined; the environment does not affect how they appear. This is also called qualitative inheritance. Type two is Continuous Variation. Here, characteristics show a complete gradation from one extreme to the other — a smooth spread, no sharp breaks. This may be due to the combined effects of environmental and genetic factors. The inheritance of the phenotype here is determined by the combined effects of many genes — called polygenes — and environmental factors. This is also known as quantitative inheritance. Examples from the chapter: human height and skin colour."

Teacher explains — Part 3: Importance of Variation (English):
"Why should we care about variation? The chapter gives us five clear reasons. One — variations make some individuals better fitted in the struggle for existence. Two — they help individuals adapt to a changing environment. Three — variation provides the genetic material for natural selection. Four — variations allow breeders to improve yield, quicker growth, increased resistance and lesser input — think about how Tamil Nadu rice breeders select better paddy varieties each generation. Five — variations constitute the raw materials for evolution. Without variation, all living things would be identical and species could not evolve or survive environmental change."
