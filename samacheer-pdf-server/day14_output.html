Teacher says — Why We Learn This:
"We are learning atavism today because it explains something farmers and plant breeders in Tamil Nadu actually notice — a crop variety that was improved over generations sometimes throws up a plant that looks exactly like the old wild ancestor. Understanding atavism tells us that ancestral genetic information is never truly deleted; it is only silenced. This helps you understand why evolution does not always move in one direction, and it gives you a powerful one-concept answer for a two-mark or three-mark exam question that most students skip because they have never studied section 2.7 carefully."

Teacher explains (English):
"Atavism is a modification of a biological structure whereby an ancestral trait reappears after having been lost through evolutionary change. The key idea is: the trait did not appear from nowhere — it was present in the ancestors, it was lost somewhere in the middle generations, and now it has come back. The best textbook example for atavism in plants is the reemergence of sexual reproduction in the flowering plant Hieracium pilosella. This plant normally reproduces asexually — but in some individuals, sexual reproduction reappears, which is exactly the ancestral mode of reproduction. That sudden reappearance is atavism. Notice: atavism is not a mutation creating something new. It is the return of something old that was hidden."

ஆசிரியருக்கு (Tamil — exact mirror):

Teacher says — Rapid Chapter Revision (English):
"Now let us sprint through the whole chapter. I will say the key idea — you complete it or give the ratio. Ready?"

"1. Mendel is the Father of Genetics. He worked from 1856 to 1863 on garden pea — Pisum sativum. He studied seven pairs of contrasting characters.

2. Law of Dominance — one allele in a pair is dominant and the other is recessive; explains the 3:1 F2 ratio in monohybrid cross.

3. Law of Segregation — alleles separate during gamete formation; each gamete gets only one allele. Gametes are never hybrid.

4. Law of Independent Assortment — genes on different chromosomes assort independently; explains the 9:3:3:1 F2 dihybrid ratio.

5. Test cross — F1 dominant crossed with homozygous recessive; ratio is 1:1:1:1 in dihybrid test cross.

6. Incomplete dominance — F2 phenotypic and genotypic ratio both 1:2:1; example Mirabilis jalapa (4 O'clock plant).

7. Codominance — both alleles expressed together; F2 ratio 1:2:1.

8. Lethal genes — Antirrhinum (snapdragon); recessive homozygous cc is lethal; modified ratio 1:2 in F2.

9. Pleiotropy — single gene affects multiple traits; example Pisum sativum (flower colour, seed colour, leaf axil spot inherited together).

10. Dominant epistasis — summer squash fruit colour; W is epistatic; F2 ratio 12:3:1.

11. Polygenic inheritance — wheat kernel colour; H. Nilsson-Ehle (1909); two genes (four alleles) control colour; bell-shaped curve; compatible with Mendel's laws.

12. Extra chromosomal inheritance — chloroplast inheritance in Mirabilis jalapa; F1 resembles the female parent; cytoplasmic genes, not nuclear genes.

13. Atavism — reappearance of ancestral trait; example Hieracium pilosella.

14. Mendel called the transmitted discrete information 'factors' — now called genes. The term 'Genetics' was introduced by W. Bateson in 1906."

[Answer: (c) Homozygous recessive]

4. Atavism in plants is best exemplified by:
(a) Mirabilis jalapa   (b) Hieracium pilosella   (c) Antirrhinum sp.   (d) Pisum sativum
[Answer: (b) Hieracium pilosella]

5. Extra nuclear inheritance follows:
(a) Mendel's law of segregation   (b) The female (maternal) parent   (c) The male parent   (d) Both parents equally
[Answer: (b) The female (maternal) parent]

6. The F2 phenotypic ratio in dominant epistasis (summer squash) is:
(a) 9:3:4   (b) 9:3:3:1   (c) 12:3:1   (d) 15:1
[Answer: (c) 12:3:1]

7. "Gametes are never hybrid" is a statement of the Law of:
(a) Dominance   (b) Independent Assortment   (c) Segregation   (d) Random Fertilization
[Answer: (c) Law of Segregation]

Section B — 2 Marks Each

8. Define atavism and give its textbook example from the chapter.

[Answer: Atavism is a modification of a biological structure whereby an ancestral trait reappears after having been lost through evolutionary change. The best textbook example is the reemergence of sexual reproduction in the flowering plant Hieracium pilosella.]

9. What is incomplete dominance? Give the phenotypic and genotypic F2 ratio in incomplete dominance.

[Answer: When one allele is not completely dominant over another allele, the F1 shows an intermediate phenotype. This is called incomplete dominance. Both phenotypic and genotypic F2 ratios are 1:2:1 (e.g., 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white in Mirabilis jalapa).]

10. Distinguish between back cross and test cross.

[Answer: Back cross — crossing F1 hybrid with either of the two parents (dominant or recessive). Test cross — crossing F1 hybrid specifically with the homozygous recessive parent to determine the genotype of the F1. Test cross is a type of back cross — specifically the recessive back cross.]

11. Name the scientist and year of the first polygenic inheritance experiment. Which plant was used and what character was studied?

[Answer: H. Nilsson-Ehle, 1909. Wheat (Triticum) — kernel colour.]

Section C — 3 Marks Each

12. Explain the Law of Independent Assortment with the F2 ratio it produces in a dihybrid cross.

[Answer: When two pairs of traits are combined in a hybrid, the segregation of one pair of alleles is independent of the segregation of the other pair. Genes located on different chromosomes assort independently during meiosis, producing all possible gamete combinations. The dihybrid F2 ratio is 9:3:3:1 (9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green in Mendel's pea experiment).]

13. Describe dominant epistasis in summer squash with the genotypes of parents, F1, and the F2 phenotypic ratio.

[Answer: White fruit parent genotype WWgg is crossed with yellow fruit parent genotype wwGG. F1 plants are white-fruited heterozygotes (WwGg) — W is dominant and epistatic, masking colour. When F1 is selfed, F2 produces a phenotypic ratio of 12 white : 3 yellow : 1 green. W masks the expression of G and g; only wwGG and wwGg plants are yellow (3/16); only wwgg plants are green (1/16); all plants with at least one W are white (12/16).]

14. Distinguish between continuous variation and discontinuous variation with one example each.

[Answer: Discontinuous variation — limited, clearly separate forms with no intermediate; controlled by one or two major genes; unaffected by environment; also called qualitative inheritance. Example: plant height in garden pea (tall or dwarf). Continuous variation — complete gradation from one extreme to another; controlled by polygenes and environmental factors; also called quantitative inheritance. Example: human height and skin colour.]

Section D — 5 Marks Each

15. Explain polygenic inheritance in wheat kernel colour with the role of R genes, the cross between dark red and white parents, F1 and F2 results, and the type of curve produced. (Use H. Nilsson-Ehle's experiment.)

[Answer: H. Nilsson-Ehle (1909) crossed dark red wheat (R1R1R2R2) with white wheat (r1r1r2r2). F1 = medium red (R1r1R2r2). F1 selfed produces F2 with a range of kernel colours determined by the number of R alleles present: 4 R alleles → dark red; 3 R alleles → medium-dark red; 2 R alleles → medium red; 1 R allele → light red; 0 R alleles → white. Each R allele contributes additively to kernel colour. The phenotypic distribution in F2 forms a bell-shaped curve showing continuous variation from dark red to white. This shows that polygenic inheritance is compatible with Mendel's laws — genes segregate and assort independently; only the phenotype appears blended, not the genes. Later a third gene was also found to affect kernel colour; with three gene pairs, Nilsson-Ehle found a 63 red : 1 white ratio in F2 with the F2 distribution 1:6:15:20:15:6:1.]

16. With a neat Punnett square, work out the F2 genotypes and phenotypes of a monohybrid cross between a pure tall pea plant (TT) and a pure dwarf pea plant (tt). State which law explains (a) the disappearance of the dwarf trait in F1 and (b) the reappearance of the dwarf trait in F2. Give the F2 genotypic and phenotypic ratios.

[Answer:
P: TT (tall) × tt (dwarf)
F1: All Tt (tall)
F1 selfed (Tt × Tt):
Punnett Square:

         T        t
  T |  TT   |   Tt  |
  t |  Tt   |   tt  |

F2 genotypes: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt — Genotypic ratio = 1:2:1
F2 phenotypes: 3 tall (TT + Tt) : 1 dwarf (tt) — Phenotypic ratio = 3:1
(a) Disappearance of dwarf in F1 — explained by the Law of Dominance: T is dominant over t, so all Tt individuals appear tall.
(b) Reappearance of dwarf in F2 — explained by the Law of Segregation: alleles T and t separate during gamete formation; tt individuals reappear when two t gametes combine. Gametes are never hybrid.]
